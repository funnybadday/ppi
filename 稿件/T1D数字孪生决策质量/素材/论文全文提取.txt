Abstract
A digital twin (DT) for type 1 diabetes (T1D) can predict how an individual’s
blood glucose will respond to different insulin treatments without actually trying
them. For each treatment, downstream statistics of predicted glucose trajectories,
such as glycemic risk, can be computed to guide clinical decisions. However, the
quality of DTs is usually measured by the accuracy of their predicted glucose tra-
jectories, which may not reflect the quality of the downstream treatment decisions
they inform. In this work, we directly evaluate DTs by this decision quality on
downstream treatment ranking, i.e., whether a DT predicts glucose trajectories that
can be used to rank candidate treatments as the real patient would. We present an
evaluation protocol over 30 virtual patients from the FDA-accepted UV A/Padova
model, each simulated under 21 different basal-bolus treatments. For each patient,
a DT is fitted under one treatment and then produces glucose trajectories for the
remaining 20 treatments. Our results show that prediction accuracy and decision
quality disagree. A baseline that models the average patient, never seeing the
individual’s glucose data, has the lowest prediction accuracy of all six methods
(RMSE: 63.6 mg/dL) but ranks treatments with the second-highest mean Spear-
man (0.766), while a black-box neural network that predicts glucose nearly twice
as accurately (RMSE: 32.9 mg/dL) ranks them no better than a random ordering
of the 20 treatments (0.049 Spearman). Therefore, selecting a DT based on pre-
diction accuracy alone can prefer one that recommends treatments at chance, and
decision quality must be evaluated directly rather than assumed to follow from
prediction accuracy.
1 Introduction
Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is an autoimmune condition in which the body stops producing insulin, requir-
ing lifelong insulin therapy and frequent monitoring of blood glucose [9]. Management of glucose
must therefore replicate the control loop that a healthy pancreas runs, usually through a constant
basal dose covering fasting needs, meal boluses proportional to carbohydrate content, and correc-
tion boluses proportional to how far glucose sits above target. In practice, this basal-bolus treatment
is specified by parameters such as the basal rate, carbohydrate ratio, and correction factor [18]. For a
fixed insulin formulation and delivery method, it is these patient-specific parameters that constitute
a treatment in our work. Choosing the treatment well is both difficult and extremely consequential
to the patient’s health. The right setting differs across patients and changes with physical activity,
illness, and time of day [9, 12, 18]. Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) has improved mat-
ters considerably, but treatment selection still relies heavily on patient-specific parameters identified
through an empirical loop: make an adjustment, observe prospective glucose values, and adjust
again.

A digital twin (DT) is a computational model that acts as the virtual stand-in for the patient [15].
For T1D, it is a personalized model that predicts how the patient’s blood glucose will respond to
insulin and carbohydrate intake, fit from their own glucose, insulin, and meal trace recorded under
the treatment they are currently on [5, 13]. Rather than testing candidate treatments directly on the
patient, the DT generates simulated glucose trajectories by applying these candidate treatments to
itself. Each trajectory is then scored by glycemic risk, which summarizes how far and how long
glucose strays from the target range [17]. Ranking the candidates by this score can give clinicians a
basis for choosing among treatment options. In other words, the DT exists to answer the counterfac-
tual question: what would happen under new treatments?
Published works evaluate DTs for T1D by prediction accuracy: how closely the predicted glucose
trajectory matches the observed one, measured by root mean squared error (RMSE) or mean absolute
relative difference (MARD) [5, 13]. However, neither measures what a DT is built to do. What
matters is decision quality: whether the trajectories a DT generates rank the candidate treatments in
the right order [1]. In fact, clinical targets are set on glycemic outcomes such as time in range [2], so
the question is whether an adjustment moves the patient in the right direction and how much better
the best alternative would have been, rather than the pointwise accuracy of a glucose forecast. High
prediction accuracy gives no guarantee that a DT’s ranking of the candidate treatments matches the
patient’s own ranking [1].
In this work, we propose an evaluation protocol that scores a DT on the decisions it supports rather
than solely on the trajectories it produces, and implement it as a benchmark on three published
methods [5, 13, 11] and three proposed baselines. The protocol is designed for DT developers
rather than for clinicians, giving evidence that a DT supports the treatment decisions a clinician
would make with it and exposing DTs that predict glucose accurately while ranking treatments
inaccurately. Each DT is fit to a patient’s glucose trajectory under a single baseline treatment and
predicts glucose under 20 counterfactual treatments. A glycemic risk kernel scores each predicted
trajectory, yielding the DT’s ranking over the 20 candidates. We utilize a cohort of virtual patients,
so that the true ranking is obtained by running every candidate on the same patient in the simulator
and applying the same kernel. Decision quality is the agreement between these rankings, measured
by Spearman correlation and regret, and is reported alongside prediction accuracy for every method.
Our contributions are:
• An evaluation protocol that scores a DT for T1D by how well its ranking of candidate treatments
agrees with the patient’s true ranking.
• A benchmark implementing this protocol on 30 UV A/Padova virtual patients and 20 counterfactual
treatments, with three published DT methods, each kept consistent with its original implementa-
tion, and three baselines.
• Evidence that the two families of metrics disagree. Methods significantly separated on prediction
accuracy are not separated on decision quality, a baseline that never reads the patient’s CGM has
the second-highest mean Spearman, and a black-box baseline that predicts glucose nearly twice
as accurately ranks treatments at chance.
2 Related Work
Digital twins for T1D.Personalized models of a patient’s glucose response are identified from
their own CGM, insulin and meal record. Physiological models are fit by Bayesian MCMC in
ReplayBG [5], by neural posterior estimation [13], or by recursive filtering [11], while black-box
models learn the map from inputs to glucose directly from data. Cappon et al. [4] compare individu-
alized black-box predictors with a physiological model and find the black-box approaches preferable
for glucose prediction, which is why our benchmark includes two black-box baselines. These works
validate a model by how closely its predictions match held-out measurements. Del Favero et al. [7]
argue that pointwise error should weigh clinically consequential mistakes more heavily and propose
a glucose-specific metric for assessing predictors, but it remains a property of a single predicted
trajectory rather than of the decision a set of trajectories supports.
Decision-aware learning.Decision-focused learning trains a predictor through the downstream
optimization so that its errors are weighted by their effect on the decision [10, 21], starting from
the observation that the most accurate predictor need not yield the best decision. Amad and van der
2
Schaar [1] carry this argument to digital twins, targeting the twin at the decisions it informs. These
works change how a model is trained. We hold training fixed at each method’s published procedure
and change only how the resulting DT is evaluated, so the protocol applies to existing T1D DT
methods without retraining them.
3 Evaluation Protocol
3.1 Setup
Let a treatmentπbe a setting of the parameters that determine a patient’s insulin delivery, letπ 0 be
the patient’s own baseline treatment, and letΠbe the set of candidate treatments, whereπ 0 /∈Π.
Running treatmentπon patientpproduces trajectory IG p,π, which a glycemic risk kernel maps to
a scalar rewardR(IG p,π). The output trajectory is defined to be noise-free interstitial glucose (IG),
rather than noisy CGM, because sensor noise describes the device rather than the patient. Ranking
ΠbyR(IG p,π)gives the patient’s ranking over the candidate treatments. With a simulated patient
cohort, we run everyπ∈Πon the same patient under the same conditions.
3.2 Digital Twins
A DT methodφ∈ Fmaps a patient’s noisy CGM, recorded under the baseline treatmentπ 0, to a
DTτ p ∈ T, soφ: CGM p,π0 7− →τp. A DT is itself a function, mapping the carbohydrate rate
dand the insulin rateu π delivered under some treatmentπto a predicted noise-free IG trajectory,
τ p : (d, u π)7− →cIGp,π. Using data recorded only underπ 0, each DT is fit once and used to
simulate glucose trajectories for every candidate treatmentπ∈Π. These two definitions are also
the only assumptions required for defining a DT method. They do not need to have specific states or
underlying physiological models, so mechanistic and black-box DT methods are scored identically.
3.3 Metrics
We evaluate a DT on the two families of metrics defined in Table 1, all computed per patient and
test day on the noise-free IG. Prediction accuracy metrics are the classic RMSE and MARD scores
between predicted and true trajectories, averaged overΠ. Decision quality metrics score the ordering
a DT induces overΠwhen its predicted trajectories are passed through the glycemic risk kernelR.
Spearman is the rank correlation between the DT’s ordering and the patient’s true ordering. Regret
scores only the top treatment that the DT recommends as the true reward it gives up against the true
best treatment, reported in both reward units and normalized acrossΠso that patients with easy
and hard decision problems are comparable. Which decision quality metric to prefer depends on
how the DT is deployed. Regret is better when a single recommended treatment goes straight to
the patient, while Spearman is better for global ranking alignment, and we choose Spearman as the
primary metric.

4 Benchmark Design
Our benchmark evaluates DT methods for T1D following the evaluation protocol defined above.
Every design choice follows asimglucosedefault [22] or a published protocol unless marked as
ours, and Table 2 attributes each choice to its source. Each method is kept consistent with its original
implementation.
4.1 Simulator and Patient Cohort
We use thesimglucosesimulator, an open-source Python implementation of the FDA-accepted
UV A/Padova T1D model [6, 22]. It has 13 states and about 30 patient-specific parameters and is
treated as a black box, mirroring real clinical scenarios where the true dynamics of blood glucose
are unknown. We extract noisy CGM for training and noise-free IG for ground truth, so measure-
ment noise is present only in training, not in evaluation. Our patient cohort consists of the 30 virtual
patients that ship withsimglucose: 10 adults, 10 adolescents, and 10 children [22]. Each patient
carries its own parameters and baseline treatment, including basal rate, carbohydrate ratio, and cor-
rection factor.

4.3 Scenario and Data
Meals are drawn fromsimglucose’s default RandomScenario: three main meals at probability0.95
and three snacks at probability0.30, yielding 1 to 6 eating occasions and196±30g of carbohydrate
intake per day. Each identification day covers 24 hours starting from 5am and CGM is sampled
every 5 minutes. Appendix A.1 provides a listing of all parameters used. Each scenario is drawn
independently and cached, so all 21 treatments and benchmarked methods see the same meals on
the same day. Differences between treatments are therefore never confounded by noise in what the
patient ate.
Training data consists of 30 independent identification days under the training treatment. We cap
it at 30 days, about one month of CGM, as a realistic amount of recent data for a single patient
and more than twice the 14 days the international consensus recommends for assessing glycemic
control [2]. We first create 29 hour trajectories, each with an independent scenario draw. The first 5
hours of each trajectory are dropped and never scored or handed to a method:simglucosetruncates
breakfast times to 5:00am–9:00am, so 5am is the earliest instant at which any meal can be drawn.
No method is required to use all 30 days, and each uses the amount its original implementation
prescribes. Evaluation data consists of 10 additional independent identification days, each starting
from the baseline treatmentπ 0 until 5am and then switching to the candidate treatment afterwards.
4.4 Methods
We evaluate six DT methods: three published DT methods for T1D, all run here on the same simpli-
fied, nine-state, eight-parameter physiological ordinary differential equation (ODE) model of Cap-
pon et al. [5], and three baselines we propose. Each method uses the amount of training data its
original implementation prescribes, so the data requirement is part of the method being evaluated.
MCMC and SBI identify from a single 22 hour window (264 CGM samples), the setting Hoang
5
et al. [13] adopt from the ReplayBG implementation and the exact input of their network, and UKF
is matched to the same window. Extending them to all 30 days is computationally expensive as every
MCMC likelihood evaluation simulates the ODE over the whole window. The single-day require-
ment is therefore a property, and a limitation, of these methods rather than of the benchmark. The
Linear and Neural baselines have no published setting, so we give them all 30 training days (8640
samples). The Prior baseline uses no training data.
•Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)[5]. The ReplayBG method: Bayesian identification
of a nine-state, eight-parameter physiological ODE by adaptive single-component Metropolis–
Hastings, followed by a multivariatet-copula over the marginals. It is identified from a single 22
hour window and replays candidate treatments from an assumed steady state.
•Simulation-based inference (SBI)[13]. A masked autoregressive flow trained by neural posterior
estimation to map a CGM trace directly to a posterior over the same ODE model, extended to infer
the nine initial conditions jointly. The flow conditions on CGM alone, so the insulin and meal
record enters only through its training set, which the original paper generates under one fixed
profile. Each patient’s record differs, so we train a fresh network per patient: 5,000 parameter-
trace pairs are simulated from the published prior under that patient’s own 22 hour record, the
flow is fit to them once, and it is then conditioned on the observed CGM to draw 1,000 posterior
samples. Nothing is shared across patients, so the benchmark evaluates the estimator rather than
the cross-patient amortization that is the original paper’s headline; its own future work targets the
same limitation by training on varied meal and insulin profiles.
•Unscented Kalman filter (UKF)[11]. In T1D, the UKF has been used to estimate plasma insulin
concentration under the Hovorka model, as a real-time estimator for artificial pancreas systems.
We apply the same joint state-parameter filtering idea to the simplified ODE and estimate glucose
rather than plasma insulin. The filter runs over the recorded inputs of the same 22 hour window,
and candidate treatments are then replayed through the same path MCMC uses.
•Linear response model (Linear). A ridge regression of CGM on lagged insulin and carbohydrate
rates, with four hours of insulin memory and three hours of carbohydrate memory. It has no
physiological model, no states, and no parameter that means anything. It regresses on the input
record alone, with no autoregressive CGM terms, because a DT must generate a full day under a
treatment whose CGM is never observed.
•Neural response model (Neural). A multilayer perceptron with two hidden layers of 64 units
over the same lagged features, training days and covariate as Linear, so the pair differs only in
model complexity and Linear’s behavior cannot be attributed to linearity. Both are black-box
models, the family that outperformed an individualized physiological model at 30 to 60 minute
glucose forecasting [4].
•Average patient (Prior). The MCMC pipeline with the identification step removed: candidate
treatments are replayed over an ensemble drawn from the published ReplayBG prior, so its identi-
fication window is empty and the DT reads no patient CGM at all. It shares the ODE, steady-state
initial condition, batched replay and ensemble median with MCMC, so it ablates exactly one in-
put. It still uses the patient’s basal insulin and body weight, without which the model cannot be
instantiated, so it isolates CGM-based identification rather than all patient information.

6 Discussion
The decision quality metrics make room for this dissociation by construction. Regret evaluatesR
on true trajectories in both of its terms, so a DT’s prediction enters only through which treatmentbπ
it selects. A DT whose predicted glucose is uniformly biased is therefore not penalized for the bias
unless the bias changes its choice, while RMSE and MARD charge for it at every sample. Spearman
extends the same property to the whole ranking: any prediction error that preserves the order of the
predicted rewards costs nothing. Prior is the extreme case. It is the least accurate predictor by a wide
margin, yet its ranking agrees with the patient’s at 0.766 Spearman, so most of its error leaves the
order of the predicted rewards intact.
The intended benefit of our work is to lower the chance of deploying a DT that predicts glucose
accurately and still recommends the wrong insulin dose. The corresponding risk is that a strong
score on this benchmark is mistaken for clinical validation, which it is not. All code is available at
https://github.com/Veeeeeee7/T1D-Digital-Twin-Benchmark.
6.1 Limitations
Glucose is clipped to[40,400]mg/dL so that regret is not dominated by a single deep excursion,
which means 39 and 15 mg/dL score identically. Of the 6000 ground-truth runs, 205 (3.4%), con-
centrated in 8 patients and almost entirely on the increased-insulin treatments, left the simulator’s
safe range and were terminated early and padded at the terminal value. The ReplayBG prior comes
from earlier studies and ReplayBG was assessed on virtual adults [5], so the 20 adolescent and child
subjects may carry extra prior mismatch. The candidate set moves a single dose axis at a time and
contains only insulin adjustments, whereas clinical adjustments may move basal and prandial doses
jointly and sometimes in opposite directions. Finally, every result is for one candidate set, one risk
kernel and a 24 hour horizon, and we do not know how much of the dissociation survives a different
objective such as time in range or a longer horizon.
7 Conclusion
We proposed evaluating T1D digital twins by the treatment decisions they support. On 30 virtual pa-
tients, prediction accuracy and decision quality disagree on which of the six methods are better, and
selecting a DT by prediction accuracy alone can prefer one that recommends treatments at chance, as
the Neural and Prior baselines show. Decision quality must therefore be reported alongside RMSE
and MARD rather than assumed to follow from them.

A.2 Existing assets
The simulator, the 30 virtual subjects, the Breton–Kovatchev CGM noise model and
RandomScenariocome fromsimglucosev0.2.11 (MIT license) [22], an independent open-source
Python implementation of the 2008 UV A/Padova simulator [16] distributed for research use. Neu-
ral posterior estimation uses thesbitoolkit v0.26.1 (Apache-2.0) [19] on PyTorch v2.12 (BSD-3-
Clause), with NumPy, SciPy and pandas (BSD-3-Clause), Gymnasium (MIT) and Matplotlib (PSF-
based Matplotlib license). The ReplayBG model, its published prior and its MCMC procedure [5],
the NPE setup of Hoang et al. [13], and the unscented Kalman filter of Hajizadeh et al. [11] are
re-implemented from the published descriptions and those papers are credited as specifications.
B Compute Resources
Every experiment runs on CPU only. All runs were submitted to an internal SLURM cluster of
64-core, 512 GB nodes, each job requesting 30 cores and 128 GB with one worker per patient, so
the cohort is fit and scored concurrently. Table 6 gives the measured cost of fitting one patient and
scoring all 20 candidate treatments on a single core, as the median over the 30 patients.
Table 6: Per-patient fit-and-score cost on one core, median over the 30 subjects.

The five fast methods ran as a single job in 861 s of wall clock, 5.5 core-hours in total. MCMC ran
separately in 8 h 30 m of wall clock, 249 core-hours. Generating the shared trajectory cache, 30
patients×30 training days plus 10 test days×20 treatments, costs a further 1182 s of wall clock,
9.9 core-hours, and is done once and reused by every method.
