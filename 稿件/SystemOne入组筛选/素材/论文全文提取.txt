A general-purpose System One decision model for clinical

Clinical care, health-system operations and biomedical discovery depend on extracting reliable answers
from patient records, yet applying diverse clinical questions across entire populations remains difficult
and computationally expensive. Task-specific models offer limited flexibility, whereas generative lan-
guage models require costly, repeated processing of lengthy records. Here we present Clinical System
One (CS1), a 4-billion-parameter decision model for fast, typed judgments from clinical records, along
with two agentic harnesses in which CS1 works together with a generalist language model for high-
volume screening. CS1-4B is trained on 12.4 million questions and answers from 3.6 million patients
across a multi-hospital health system. Training combines structured-data keys, observed outcomes
and language-model supervision to support questions across clinical notes, diagnostic reports and
longitudinal patient timelines. CS1-4B answers multiple questions with predefined response options in a
single forward pass and accommodates diverse questions and criteria without task-specific retraining.
Each answer carries a probability that can route uncertain cases to human review, and on-demand
attribution to the individual sentences it rests on. On 963,408 held-out patients, CS1-4B outperformed
open decision models up to 27 billion parameters on 13 of 14 tasks scored independently of its training
teacher. Across 21 external data sets, its aggregate performance approached that of the strongest tested
27-billion-parameter model, with 6.4-fold faster inference in a matched workload. A single desktop
graphics processor reads about 2,000 notes per hour at 40 questions each, allowing deployment on
modest local hardware. Integrating CS1-4B with a language model agent extended these capabilities
from individual documents to longitudinal chart review and population screening: the combined system
produced cited answers over complete multi-year patient histories and screened a patient population
against the eligibility criteria of 17 registered clinical trials. In the retrospective trial-screening analy-
sis, this approach placed four times as many judged-eligible patients among its top 20 candidates as
keyword search and reduced language-model token use 690-fold relative to a language model reading
every record. These findings support a division of labour in clinical artificial intelligence, with small
decision models performing repeated record reading and generative models formulating questions and
synthesizing evidence, and provide a foundation for continuously queryable clinical records supporting
registry abstraction, quality measurement, cohort discovery, trial screening and clinical review.

Clinical records support a large volume of fast, narrow judgements. Trial teams and sponsors screen thousands
of candidates against dozens of eligibility criteria1,2. Registries and cohort studies ask one phenotype question
of every patient in a health system3. Payers and coding teams review whether the record supports what was
billed or requested, quality programmes audit every discharge for the same measures4, and clinicians reviewing
a chart ask how a problem evolved over years of notes5. Each question is simple for a clinician who is familiar
with the patient context, but across whole populations or long records, the cost quickly balloons beyond a
feasible effort threshold. In Kahneman’s terms these are System 1 judgements, fast and typed, as opposed to
the deliberate System 2 reasoning of diagnosis and treatment planning6.
Established automated workflows do not fit this workload. Clinical natural-language processing pipelines7,8
and supervised phenotyping or risk models 9,10 are fast but fixed to the concepts they were built for. Large
language models (LLMs) accept questions in natural language11–14, but every question is a separate generation,
often with explicit reasoning15, so cost grows with patients × questions × record length. Caching a shared
record prefix16,17 or asking several questions in one prompt18 reduces the repeated reading, but every answer is
still generated token by token. Their answers come without calibrated probabilities that could route uncertain
cases to a human for review19,20, and rarely with evidence a reviewer can check without rereading the chart21.
Data-use agreements for clinical text also often rule out hosted models22,23, limiting local usage to small LLMs
that have lower accuracy and higher hallucination rates.
A new class of open models targets exactly this interface. “System One” decision models take a state and a set
of typed questions, and return an independent probability distribution for every question in one forward pass24.
JEV-27B25, Clef26 and the Decision 2.0 family of 0.6–27-billion-parameter models27 are publicly available.
They are trained on public and synthetic data, however, and none reports an evaluation on clinical records.
An independent medical evaluation, of the hosted Jev service on examination and case-report benchmarks,
found it fast and accurate on literature questions but overconfident and behind a reasoning LLM on complex
diagnosis, and recommended verifying and recalibrating its probabilities for every task 28. A second study
tested Jev as a judge of the factual accuracy of generated radiology reports29, and small open decision models
for medicine have begun to appear30–32. A team that wants to screen a population or review charts with such a
model needs to know whether it reads real records reliably, whether its probabilities can decide which cases
need review, and whether its answers can be checked.
The distribution of a patient population in a real hospital system may look very different from the curated
clinical data sets that are publicly available. Institutional clinical records can supply what public corpora
cannot: decades of real patient trajectories and clinical text. Structured data give the exact answer to many
questions about a record, and longitudinal follow-up gives the observed outcome of every prediction. General-
purpose decision models have yet to be thoroughly evaluated and adapted for clinical question answering on
real patient records. Even if models trained on a health system’s own records predict outcomes well10,33,34,
they may fail to transfer to other sites or remain calibrated there35–38. In order to become trusted for real-world
use, clinical models must be evaluated not only on held-out patient data from within a healthcare system
(to assess its performance if deployed locally), but also show generalization to other hospitals with different
patient populations.
Here we present Clinical System One (CS1-4B), a System One decision model for clinical records trained on
Klaros-12M: more than 12 million decisions about 3.6 million patients at MGB, a multi-hospital US academic
health system (Fig. 1). We evaluate CS1-4B from single decisions to whole populations. We first establish
that CS1-4B makes individual decisions reliably and efficiently, by assessing its accuracy and calibration on
held-out MGB patients (MGBQA-1M) and on 21 public data sets from other institutions, against 13 publicly
available decision models ranging from 0.6B to 27B parameters. We then show that each of CS1’s answers
about a note or patient timeline can be traced to the passages it rests on, forming the basis of our evidence

attribution pipeline. Finally, we show that a generalist LLM using CS1 as a tool can answer questions about a

each record.
Results
Clinical System One (CS1) design and training.CS1 reads one document with any number of typed
questions and returns a probability distribution over each question’s options (Fig. 1b,c). Questions (choices
among named options, ordered scores or yes/no claims, each option with a criterion for boundary cases) are
written at request time, so a new criterion, phenotype or outcome definition needs no retraining. Documents
are clinical notes, diagnostic reports, multi-note records and structured patient timelines rendered as text up to
a decision point (Methods).
CS1-4B was initialized from Decision 2.0 Nox-4B27 and trained on 12,416,448 document–question pairs from
3,643,798 MGB patient records (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Table 1). Its labels are of three kinds (Fig. 1d):
option probabilities read out of an LLM teacher, GLM-5.3-Flash39, on institutional hardware (Extended Data
Fig. 1); exact answers computed by rules from structured data; and outcomes observed after the decision point,
such as readmission. Its fine-tuning used no records from other institutions and no public data set.
The document is encoded once, and each question is a short segment that attends to the document and to itself
but never to another question, so each answer equals that of a single-question prompt and an extra question
costs only its own tokens (Fig. 1e and Extended Data Fig. 2). On one NVIDIA GB10 desktop graphics
processor (commonly referred to as a DGX Spark), CS1-4B answered 40 questions about a 2,900-token note in
1.80 s, against 11.54 s for Decision 2.0 Vega-27B (6.4 times faster); the Decision 2.0 models were run through
their released inference code and not further optimized (Extended Data Table 2 and Methods). The speed-ups
held for documents of 512 to 16,384 tokens, and show the efficiency of asking multiple questions in a single
pass: reading the document once per question would have taken CS1-4B 15 times longer (Extended Data
Fig. 3).
Performance on held-out patients.On real patient records, we compared CS1-4B with ten open deci-
sion models: the six Decision 2.0 models, JEV-27B, Clef, pplx-decider-v1-27b 40 and BioDecision-4B30;
MedDecider-9B and -27B31 were evaluated on the public data sets only (Methods). Each baseline model
was run through its official implementation with a question input format that was optimized for that model.
Tasks fall into six task types, three read from structured records and three from text (Fig. 2a). Because always
giving the most common answer scores well in raw accuracy on several tasks, the primary metric is balanced
accuracy, the mean recall over answer classes.
On MGBQA-1M (28 tasks; 963,408 held-out MGB patients, one question each; Extended Data Table 3),
CS1-4B was the most accurate decision model in five of the six task types (Fig. 2b). It led on 13 of the 14
tasks whose keys come from rules, observed outcomes or an open LLM: it reached 96.6% averaged over the
eight timeline tasks, against 86.8% for JEV-27B, the best open model there; 92.1% on the clinical-definition
task (Vega-27B 85.0%); 75.6% on outcome forecasting (JEV-27B 69.4%); and 95.0% on medication status
(BioDecision-4B 82.6%). The exception was cancer staging from diagnostic reports, where pplx-decider-
v1-27b reached 86.9% against 86.6%. Averaged over the six task types, CS1-4B scored 89.7, well above its
initialization, Nox-4B (73.3), and above Vega-27B (81.7), the best open model (Fig. 2b). CS1-4B thus read
the real-world care trajectories of held-out patients accurately, whereas the open models generalized poorly
to institutional data. MGBQA-1M is also the only evaluation in this study whose records and keys no open
model could have seen, because they are restricted to institutional systems. Among the 14 tasks keyed by

Generalization to external data sets.We evaluated the ability of CS1-4B to transfer its institutionally
trained knowledge to public data sets. The 21 public data sets come from other institutions: timelines
rendered from the structured records of MIMIC-IV 41,42, eICU43 and EHRSHOT44, and public notes and
reports (Fig. 2a,c, Extended Data Table 4 and Methods). Averaged over the six task types, CS1-4B scored 86.7,
against 81.7 for its initialization, Nox-4B, and 88.6 for Vega-27B, the strongest open model, while answering
6.4 times faster; it was above Vega-27B on patient timelines, outcome forecasting and questions over notes. A
27-billion-parameter model was the best open model on 20 of the 21 sets.
CS1-4B exceeded every open model, even the 27-billion-parameter models, on 5 sets, most clearly on
structured records: 92.8% on eICU-Timelines and 94.3% on EHRSHOT-Timelines, against 91.1% and 90.5%
for JEV-27B, the best open model on both, and a mean AUROC of 0.766 on EHRSHOT-Prognosis, the
best outcome ranking of any system (Clef, 0.681). It trailed the best open model by 7.4–12.8 points on
the three clinical-definition sets, ranked outcomes below every 27-billion-parameter model on MIMIC- and
eICU-Prognosis, and, of the eleven sets read from notes and reports, led narrowly on MIPA and BRIE but
trailed on the other nine, by 1.1–5.9 points. These leads are not an artefact of its training layout: on MIMIC-IV
timelines rendered both ways, CS1-4B, like every other model, scored higher in the encounter layout it was
not trained on (Extended Data Table 5). Training on MGB records also lifted CS1-4B over its initialization on
these sets, by 12.6 points on patient timelines, 7.1–7.2 points on clinical definitions and outcome forecasting,
and−0.9 to 3.6 points on the three task types read from notes and reports (Fig. 2c,e).
Because the training data of most open models are undisclosed, we cannot exclude that the published
benchmarks, or the records on which any of the sets are based, were seen in their training45, so their scores
may partly reflect exposure to the underlying records. Thus the public datasets primarily serve to show
how CS1-4B’s finetuning on internal MGB data transfers to improved performance over Nox-4B at other
institutions, and are less useful as a direct comparator between CS1-4B and other public models.
Calibrated probabilities at population scale.At population scale, probabilities decide which answers are
accepted automatically and which a person reviews (Fig. 1f). CS1-4B results are first reported without an
explicit calibration stage on validation data. On the public data sets, the expected calibration error (ECE) of its
top answer was 0.012–0.052 across the six task types, below that of its initialization, Nox-4B, in every task
type (0.033–0.139; Fig. 2h). Pooled over five human-keyed public benchmarks (14,756 items), its ECE was
0.029, about half that of Nox-4B (0.058), but above that of JEV-27B (0.010) and Clef (0.024), and per set
it ranged from 0.007 to 0.100 (Extended Data Table 2). Its outcome forecasts rank patients effectively, but
absolute risks did not transfer: over the 27 outcomes of the three public prognosis sets, its mean predicted
risk was 0.39 against an observed incidence of 0.19, and the calibration error of its predicted risks was 0.203
(Fig. 2g), as expected where intensive-care or emergency cohorts have baseline incidences unlike those of a
general hospital population. Within each outcome, however, its predictions had about the right spread (median
calibration slope 1.03), so one correction per outcome sufficed: shifting each outcome’s predicted risks on the
log-odds scale to match its incidence46, estimated on the other half of the patients, reduced the calibration
error to 0.014 and the Brier score from 0.182 to 0.110, below that of Nox-4B after the same correction (0.118).
A site that knows the incidence of its outcomes can thus read CS1-4B’s forecasts as risks without retraining.

Sentence-level evidence attribution.To identify which parts of a clinical record drive CS1’s answers, we
measured how the predicted probability changes when individual sentences are removed or considered in
isolation47,48 (Fig. 3a). We used two complementary measures, necessity and sufficiency. Necessity is defined
as the decrease in probability when a sentence is removed from the record; sufficiency, in contrast, is defined
as the increase in probability when that sentence is presented alone, relative to an empty document. Together,
these measures distinguish evidence that is essential to an answer from evidence that independently supports
it, even when the same clinical fact appears in multiple places.
We quantified the faithfulness of the attributions on the 90 note–question pairs whose answer depends on the
note (probability below 0.5 for an empty document), among 200 pairs from 100 MIMIC-IV discharge notes
not used in training (Fig. 3b,c). Removing the three highest-ranked sentences changed the model’s answer in
41% of cases when ranked by necessity and 59% when ranked by sufficiency, compared with 1% for randomly
selected sentences. Conversely, retaining only the highest-ranked sentences recovered a mean probability
of 0.89 for the original answer, compared with 0.19 for random selections. These results indicate that the
identified sentences have a substantially greater influence on the model’s decisions than other, arbitrary parts
of the record. Attribution incurs additional computation, with median processing times of 24 s per answer for
necessity and 10 s for sufficiency on a single NVIDIA GB10 machine. We therefore compute attribution on
demand, allowing reviewers to inspect the sentences underlying selected decisions without incurring this cost
for every answer in a population-scale screen.
We next examined attribution in synthetic notes with known supporting evidence (Fig. 3d–f). For a patient
presenting with dyspnoea, CS1 identified an explicit denial of chest pain with probability 0.98. Removing
the sentence documenting the denial reduced this probability to 0.07, identifying it as the principal evidence
for the answer (Fig. 3d). In a second example, empagliflozin was documented both in the hospital course
and in the discharge medication list. Either mention independently supported the answer that the patient was
prescribed an SGLT2 inhibitor at discharge, while removing either had little effect because the other remained
(Fig. 3e). More complex questions required evidence distributed across the record. For a structured patient
timeline, creatinine rose from 0.9 to 2.4 mg/dL before returning to 1.0 mg/dL. The model identified this course
as rising and recovering, with the largest contributions coming from laboratory measurements on different
days rather than a single observation (Fig. 3f). Extended Data Fig. 4 shows further cases, including an answer
resting on silence, and the limit of single-sentence attribution: evidence repeated across days makes no line
necessary, and a pattern across days is carried by no single line.
Question answering over longitudinal patient records.Whole-record question answering is a routine but
labour-intensive part of chart review5. In order to answer a question about a patient’s history, a reviewer must
find every relevant note, report and structured result across years of care, and LLMs answering questions
over long or whole records49,50 often omit information spread across documents51. General LLMs plan and

course of cancer. Because the LLM plans from the manifest and never reads the record itself, the LLM’s cost
does not grow with the length of the record, and every statement in the answer points to a bounded segment of
the raw record.
To assess how well the pipeline answered, each answer was checked against its full record by GLM-5.3 at
high reasoning effort55 and by reading short excerpts (Methods). Our three answers were correct in substance
with minor errors (one of them due to a mistake in the raw record). In the kidney example, the worst level
of kidney function came from the indication line of a radiology request that listed end-stage renal disease,
which the rest of the record contradicts, and two statements repeated errors of the aggregated answers (a count
of documents and a conflict built on cysts misread as masses). The anticoagulation answer omitted a left
ventricular assist device, a further indication for anticoagulation. It also missed the documented site of the
index admission’s bleed and flagged a conflict with head CT reports that addressed only intracranial bleeding.
The cancer answer stated progression more firmly than mixed recent findings support, listed a radiosensitizing
course of cisplatin as systemic therapy and a bone marrow transplant that rests on a single billing code, and
omitted several regimens. Most of these errors are easily flagged in the answer. The end-stage renal disease
rests on a request’s indication line and the bleeding conflict on head CT reports, so a reviewer can catch and
correct them by opening a few sentences rather than rereading the whole record.
Clinical trial eligibility screening across a patient population.Screening is a primary bottleneck in trial
development2. In order to ensure participant safety and reliable scientific results, researchers must check
and rank every candidate against predefined eligibility criteria written in free-form text. This process can
be tedious59, especially given dozens of criteria and a database of thousands or millions of patients. LLMs
that read clinical notes have screened patients for individual trials 60–62 and, in a randomized trial, sped up
prescreening59, but they read each candidate’s record for every trial. To solve this, we introduce an automated
screening pipeline that accelerates and streamlines patient selection. We frame screening as a ranking problem:
Given a list of trial criteria and a population of patients, a trial screening system must produce a ranking of the
patients such that higher-ranked patients are more likely to be eligible. Our method uses an LLM (GLM-5.3)
to translate a trial’s free-text eligibility criteria into a series of atomic typed questions, each bound to a specific
timeframe, much as earlier systems translated criteria into cohort queries63. CS1-4B then does high-volume
question answering by scanning every medical document for every candidate. We aggregate these answers
into a standardized eligibility score (Fig. 5a).
We tested this pipeline on 1,000 test patients from the MIMIC-IV database (21,866 medical documents),
each screened for 17 trials from ClinicalTrials.gov 64, and evaluated its accuracy at two levels: individual
criteria matching (tested on 7 trials and 200 patients) and overall patient ranking (tested across the 13 trials
whose judged pool contained an eligible or possibly eligible patient). For criteria that could be answered using
rule-based calculations from structured data, our pipeline matched the raw LLM, which read every single
record: our pipeline achieved a balanced accuracy of 0.884 compared to the LLM’s 0.869 (difference 0.015,

To grade how well the pipeline ranked whole patients by multiple criteria, a blinded LLM judge55 reviewed
the full medical records of the top 20 patients selected by each method across the 13 trials whose judged pool
contained an eligible or possibly eligible patient. Performance was measured using nDCG@20 (Normalized
Discounted Cumulative Gain)58, a metric from 0 to 1 that rewards placing judged-eligible patients at the very
top of the list; 1 is the best ordering of the judged patients, 0 is assigned to a top 20 without any. Our pipeline
achieved a mean nDCG@20 of 0.64, significantly outperforming standard BM25 keyword search, which
scored just 0.11. Our method beat keyword search in 12 out of the 13 trials (Fig. 5d), and on average placed
3.5 judged-eligible patients in its top 20 list per trial, compared to just 0.8 patients for the keyword search.
These grades are judgements by the same LLM (GLM-5.3) that wrote the questions and keyed the note-only
criteria, and LLM judges can favour their own outputs65; the weighted κ57 of 0.84 between its two grading
passes measures consistency, not agreement with clinicians.
Our pipeline is exceptionally cost-effective, using 690 times fewer LLM tokens per patient to rank candidates
compared to the pure LLM approach, which required reading every record (Fig. 5e). Note that this token count
excludes the internal processing of CS1-4B, which generated roughly 4.0 million answers by evaluating all 519
trial questions (17 trials) across 7,695 individual record units for the 1,000 patients. Because the benchmark
LLM (which reads every record) is so expensive, we ran it as a baseline for 200 patients at the individual
criterion level; ranking all 1,000 patients would be cost-prohibitive. Consequently, the final 1,000-patient
ranking comparison was conducted against the much cheaper keyword search.
Discussion
CS1 makes it practical to ask many narrow questions of every record in a large patient population. For users
who read records at scale, this changes what is affordable. A trial team can have every candidate in a large
population ranked within days, with an LLM reading only the criteria rather than every record. A clinician
can ask a question about a patient’s whole history and receive an answer whose every statement points to a
sentence in the chart. A registry can extract structured definitions from clinical free-text without extensive
manual curation; confident answers can be auto-accepted and uncertain ones can be routed to a reviewer.
These use cases are enabled by CS1’s training on real-world patient records. On held-out patients of the
multi-hospital MGB system, it was the most accurate decision model tested in five of six task types. On public
data sets from other institutions, it led every open model on the structured timelines of eICU and EHRSHOT
and on outcome forecasting in EHRSHOT, gained most over its initialization where records are structured,
and had about half the calibration error of its initialization. Averaged over the six task types, it came within 2
points of the strongest 27-billion-parameter model, Vega-27B, which, at 27 billion parameters, has 6.4 times
the latency of CS1-4B.
For a team deciding whether to use CS1, these results suggest the following. First, it is most reliable where the
records are structured and the keys exact, and on the institution’s own tasks; on most public note and report sets
the larger open models were better, so criteria read from free text deserve more review. Second, its outcome
forecasts rank patients well and can be easily recalibrated to the local incidence of each outcome. Third, the
cost is low enough to ask many questions of every patient: a small desktop GPU reads about 2,000 notes an
hour at 40 questions each, and a more powerful machine would make this many times faster. Fourth, every
answer can be traced to the sentences it rests on. Finally, CS1 composes effectively with general LLMs, which
keep the flexibility of language while delegating population-scale reading to a small, fast decision model:
12

given CS1 as a tool, an LLM answered questions about whole patient records with cited sentences and ranked
patients for real trials while reading none of the records itself.
Rapid decision models such as CS1 are complementary to foundation models of the electronic health
record66,67. Apollo 68, trained on 25 billion events from 7.2 million patients of the same health system,
compresses a patient’s multimodal history into one representation that supports forecasting of disease onset,
progression and treatment response, and similarity search across a population. Such a representation answers
what is likely to happen to a patient and which patients resemble one another; each endpoint is read out by a
model fitted for it, and attributions point to learned associations across events. CS1 answers a different kind of
question: what the record states, posed in words at request time without task-specific training, with a calibrated
probability and the sentences that support it. The two can work together. CS1 can turn criteria written in words
into labels, cohorts and endpoints on which representation models are fitted and evaluated, and can check,
sentence by sentence, the patients that a representation model forecasts to be at risk or retrieves as similar; a
representation model can, in turn, shortlist the patients that CS1 reads.
The comparison also shows what open models do well. The 27-billion-parameter models were the best open
model on 20 of the 21 public data sets and read notes, reports, long multi-document records and assertion
status well. Their failures cluster on content that institutional supervision covers: time windows, laboratory
thresholds, definitions and outcomes. CS1-4B closed this gap on the eICU and EHRSHOT timelines but not
on the MIMIC-IV timelines or on clinical definitions. Part of the open models’ advantage may also come from
training data we cannot audit: of the open models, only JEV-27B’s corpus is public, and we found no MIMIC
text in it (Methods), whereas the Decision 2.0 and Clef corpora are undisclosed. The gap ran the other way on
institutional data: the open models, which may have seen the public data sets in training, generalized poorly to
the real-world care trajectories of MGB patients, where CS1-4B led in five of six task types. This is the setting
that matters for deployment. Most hospitals’ own records, with their diverse structured data and free text from
routine care, are likely to resemble MGB’s more closely than curated research databases such as MIMIC-IV ,
eICU-CRD and EHRSHOT.
This study has important limitations. First, the data curation depends on design choices regarding timeline,
definition and forecasting sets, which were built from each source’s records and rendered as text by a rule-
based serializer, and whose rule keys define each concept exactly as posed. Although we aimed at universal
questions, these can differ in clinical intent, billing codes may lack timing and severity69, and outcomes may
stay unobserved when care happened elsewhere 70, as, for example, in MIMIC-IV , which mostly includes
emergency department and ICU visits. Second, three public keys are written by models, HISTAI’s by our
teacher, BRIE’s by an open LLM with its authors’ code and ReXVQA’s by its authors’ LLM pipeline, as are the
14 secondary teacher-keyed MGBQA-1M tasks. An LLM with reasoning agreed with human keys on 94–98%
of items on three benchmarks but on only 90.6% of phenotype judgements, below the most common answer,
because its notion of a phenotype was broader than the annotators’ (Methods), so model-written keys can
diverge systematically from clinical annotation71. Third, CS1 is built for reading judgements over records, not
for differential diagnosis from case descriptions, where System One models trail reasoning LLMs28. Finally,
CS1 reads what records state and forecasts what records predict. If information is missing from the context
provided to CS1, its answers may be materially affected. CS1 is a tool for research, operations and review
with a person in the loop, has not been validated prospectively and must not make autonomous decisions about
individual patients.
CS1-4B is the first Clinical System One model. Its weights will follow as a credentialed release upon
institutional approval. Future versions of CS1 will also read medical images, such as radiographs and
pathology slides, alongside clinical text and structured records. We expect calibrated, evidence-linked answers
13

to typed questions to become a standard layer between clinical records and the many programs, people and
agents that need to read them at scale.
Methods
Public evaluation data.The 21 public data sets cover the six task types of Fig. 2a and are used for evaluation
only (documents and scored pairs, Extended Data Table 4). We built the nine timeline, definition and
forecasting sets from MIMIC-IV , eICU-CRD and EHRSHOT, the MedAgentBench records, BRIE and the two
pathology sets; the others are published benchmarks converted to our question format without changing their
keys. CS1-4B was fine-tuned only on Klaros-12M data, so none of the 19,027 patients behind the scored pairs
contributed a fine-tuning record.
Structured records.Patients come from MIMIC-IV v3.1 41,72, eICU-CRD v2.043,73 (intensive-care admis-
sions at 208 US hospitals) and EHRSHOT44. Each document is one patient’s structured record up to a decision
point, rendered as text by a deterministic rule-based serializer with only relative time printed, in the encounter
layout (MIMIC-IV sets; the index admission day by day, earlier admissions summarized) or the timeline
layout (eICU-CRD and EHRSHOT sets; periods counted back from the index day, recent days in full and older
periods summarized); the two layouts are compared on the same MIMIC-IV documents in Extended Data
Table 5. Documents fit 7,400 Qwen3 tokens (8,000 for MIMIC- and EHRSHOT-Timelines), the oldest history
being shortened first. Keys are computed by rules on the structured record or are outcomes observed after the
decision point. A rule is used only if the layout prints every field it reads, and a rule key is kept only when the
rule applied to what the rendered text shows gives the same answer as on the full record. eICU-CRD tables are
mapped to MIMIC-IV-shaped tables, so that the same rules, definitions, outcomes and question wording apply,
and one stay is drawn per adult patient with hospital quotas proportional to the square root of eligible patients.
Patient timelines and clinical definitions.MIMIC-Timelines and EHRSHOT-Timelines pose template-
generated questions (existence in a window, thresholds, trends, counts, orderings and others) with fixed
option sets; a “no” is keyed only when the relevant part of the record is shown in full, answers are balanced
per template, and a seeded subset of test documents is scored with 40 questions each. MIMIC-Composite
poses 22 published composite definitions as Choices whose option criteria state the definition’s logic in
plain language: eight eMERGE/PheKB phenotypes 74, six Charlson and Elixhauser comorbidity groups 75,
two NHSN infection-surveillance categories76, five laboratory definitions and one stroke quality measure.
eICU-Composite poses the 17 of them that port to eICU-CRD, and EHRSHOT-Composite curated published
definitions and scores, with answers balanced per kind. eICU-Timelines and the Composite sets score one
question per patient.
Outcome forecasting.Each question asks about an event in an explicit window after a cut in the record,
which shows only facts recorded by the cut; one outcome is asked per patient, at natural prevalence. MIMIC-
Prognosis cuts at discharge or 24 h after admission and asks twelve yes/no questions (outcomes of all three sets,
Extended Data Table 6); a death before the event counts as “no”, and windows that end after the patient’s data
end are dropped. eICU-Prognosis cuts 24 h after hospital admission or at the first intensive-care discharge and
asks about six events in the rest of the stay, because eICU-CRD records nothing after discharge. EHRSHOT-
Prognosis cuts at an index day and asks each patient one question in 22 kinds; the two kinds without a single
14

event option are reported separately. The headline is the mean AUROC77 over the outcomes with at least 10
positives and 10 negatives among the scored pairs (Extended Data Table 6). The three sets differ in cuts and
outcomes and are not comparable with one another.
MedAgentBench records.From the de-identified FHIR records of 100 patients shipped with MedAgent-
Bench78, we rendered timelines at 1–6 cut points per patient and posed Choices in eight families, keyed by exact
rules with the visibility check; a “no” also requires every list in the window to be complete. Answer-balanced
selection kept at most 22 pairs per question.
BRIE.We regenerated BRIE chart-review questions 51 on MIMIC-IV-Note v2.242 with the authors’ code and
an open LLM (Gemma-4-26B-A4B79,80), which wrote items and keys from each patient’s earlier discharge
summaries and radiology reports; each item became a Choice with a not-documented option.
Published benchmarks.MIMIC-IV-Ext-Apixaban-Trial-Criteria 81 (numeric criteria banded at trial thresh-
olds), EHRNoteQA82,83, LongHealth50, MIMIC-III phenotypes9,84–86, MIMIC-CXR-VQA87–89 (presence
questions on MIMIC-CXR90 reports) and ReXVQA91 (a seeded stratified subset). Both radiograph sets are
asked of the written report only (for MIMIC-CXR-VQA, findings the report does not mention count as absent),
so the results are not comparable with image-based VQA.
Pathology reports.Both sets come from the test split of a labelled collection of public pathology reports.
TCGA92: optical-character-recognition text of one diagnostic report per patient93 and 31 questions (histology,
stage, grade, receptor status and others), keyed by the patient’s Genomic Data Commons94 records where the
keyed value is stated in the report; a review of 196 pairs found no key errors. HISTAI95: 1,000 seeded cases
(English translations) without an independent key, so the key is the teacher’s rotation-averaged answer, the
teacher is not scored, and CS1, distilled from that teacher, has a built-in advantage; a confident subset (teacher
probability≥0.9) is reported separately.
Phenotypes and assertions.Each note–phenotype pair of MIPA 96 (16 phenotypes in MIMIC-IV discharge
summaries) is a two-option Choice whose criteria paraphrase MIPA’s definitions. Each note–entity pair of van
Aken et al.97 (mentions in MIMIC-III notes labelled present, possible or absent) is a Choice with a further
not-documented option; entities with conflicting labels are dropped, and balanced accuracy averages the three
class recalls.

Reasoning annotator.To measure how far a model-written key can be trusted, GLM-5.3 52, a mixture-of-
experts LLM with 744 billion parameters (40 billion active), with reasoning and greedy decoding (one question
per request, accepting only an explicit option letter) answered the human-keyed benchmarks. It agreed with
the human keys on 94.0% of Apixaban judgements, 97.9% of EHRNoteQA items and 94.4% of LongHealth
items, but on only 90.6% of MIMIC-III phenotype judgements, below the most common answer (92.5%): it
called phenotypes more liberally than the annotators.
Model.CS1-4B is a full fine-tune of Decision 2.0 Nox-4B 27, a Qwen3.5103 hybrid of 4.2 billion parameters
(24 Gated DeltaNet linear-attention layers 104 and 8 gated full-attention layers 105), and keeps that model’s
runtime prompt and candidate head. Every question is typed: a Choice over 2–11 options, a yes/no question, or
a Score with ordered levels 1 to N; not_documented is offered wherever the document can be silent. The
prompt holds the document, the question and one block per option, with its key and criterion; yes/no questions
take the runtime’s yes/no type, and Choices and Scores its choice type, with options keyed A, B, C, . . . . A
question has at most 11 options. The candidate head layer-normalizes the hidden states at the last token of
every option block and at the final token of the question, projects them with option- and query-side MLPs into
16

a 256-dimensional key and query space and returns one logit per option; a softmax over the question’s options
gives the answer distribution. One packed sequence holds the document once, followed by one segment per
question whose position indices restart at the end of the document, so that the document and any one segment
are token for token the runtime’s input for that question (Extended Data Fig. 2). In the full-attention layers a
block mask (FlexAttention106) lets each segment attend to the whole document and causally to itself, never to
another segment. The Gated DeltaNet layers run the document once and start every segment from its final
recurrent state, recomputing the four-token causal convolution at each segment’s start with the document’s
last tokens as left context. This extends packing without cross-contamination 107 to a shared prefix in both
layer types; training packs several documents per sequence. The document keeps at most 15,356 tokens, a
segment at most 1,024 (option descriptions are shortened first), and a document with its segments at most
20,480; documents with more questions are split over units that repeat the document. One temperature per
question type and number of options can be fitted on Klaros-12M validation documents108; CS1-4B’s results
are raw probabilities.
Training.Each example is one document with all of its labelled questions, as at inference. The loss is the
soft cross-entropy between the student’s distribution and the target, a strictly proper scoring rule109, averaged
over questions110; the target is the teacher’s readout distribution or, where an exact or outcome label exists
for the same document–question pair, the one-hot answer. Option order is shuffled per example for Choices.
Only Klaros-12M training-split documents are used. Backbone and head were fully fine-tuned with fused
AdamW111 (β1 =0.9 , β2 =0.98 , ε=10 −6, weight decay 0.01), with gradient norms clipped at 1.0, in three
consecutive stages. The first ran 13,591 optimizer steps on the main Klaros-12M training corpus, weighted
towards structured timelines, at a peak learning rate of 7×10 −6. The second continued for 11,709 steps on the
same corpus, weighted towards notes and reports, re-annealing the learning rate from 3.6×10 −6 to 7×10 −7.
The third ran 1,141 steps with a fresh optimizer on a set of multi-note documents, with the learning rate
decaying from 3×10 −6 to 3×10 −7. In total, the 26,441 steps covered about 13.8 billion training tokens and
took 26.4 h on eight NVIDIA B300 GPUs of an institutional cluster (211 GPU-hours).

Patient-level question answering.From a manifest of the record (document types, counts and dates), the
LLM writes typed sub-questions, each with a not_documented option, an aggregation rule (any, latest,
trajectory, majority or count) and a scope; CS1-4B answers every sub-question over every document in scope,
chunking long documents; code aggregates across documents and time, separating documented absence from
silence and flagging conflicts; sufficiency attribution selects evidence sentences in the decisive documents
(ssuf ≥1 nat and p(a|sentence alone)≥0.5 ); and the LLM, which never reads the full record, composes
the answer from cited sentences and aggregates, with citations validated against the evidence list. The three
worked examples (Fig. 4 and Extended Data Figs. 5 and 6) are MIMIC-IV test patients chosen from the 100
longest test records by structured signals so that each question applies; each is a single run, checked against
the full record by GLM-5.3 at high reasoning effort and by reading short excerpts.
Evidence attribution.Let u1, . . . ,um be a document’s units (sentences; for a structured record, the lines
of its rendered timeline) and a the model’s top answer to question q. The necessity score of a unit is
snec(ui) =logp(a|d,q)−logp(a|d\u i,q), where d\u i is the document with ui deleted and re-encoded; the
sufficiency score, ssuf(ui) =logp(a|u i,q)−logp(a|∅,q) , compares the unit alone with an empty document
18

and also finds redundantly stated evidence 47,115. Fig. 3 shows the corresponding probability differences,
which order the units identically. Because deleting ui leaves the text before it unchanged, the model’s state
at ui (full-attention keys and values, Gated DeltaNet recurrent states and convolution inputs) is computed
once and reused116, and only the rest of the document and the question are re-encoded; this is exact up to
floating-point rounding. Documents of more than 34 units are screened hierarchically: chunks of 10 units
are deleted one at a time, and the units of the three chunks whose deletion lowers logp(a) most are then
scored singly; every reported score is exact. Up to three units with snec ≥0.1 are listed as evidence. When
no unit is necessary and p(a|∅,q)<0.5 , units with p(a|u i,q)≥0.5 and ssuf ≥1 are listed as redundant
evidence; when p(a|∅,q)≥0.5 the answer rests on silence and nothing is highlighted. Faithfulness was
evaluated on 200 pairs from 100 MIMIC-IV discharge notes of at most 3,000 tokens (per note, one question
whose teacher answer is not its most common answer and one at random; evidence-dependent, the 90 with
p(a|∅)<0.5 ): the top k∈ {1,2,3,5,10} units by necessity or sufficiency were deleted (deletion) or kept
alone (insertion)47,117, the area under each curve is the original answer’s mean probability over k, and two
random orderings serve as baseline; results are for the 90 evidence-dependent pairs.
Metrics and statistics.Accuracy is computed on the top option. Balanced accuracy 118 is the mean recall
over the key’s answer classes per question, averaged over questions (questions with fewer than 20 items, and
item-specific options, are pooled by answer position); on eICU-Timelines and the three Composite sets it is
computed per concept and averaged over concepts. Calibration is reported as top-label expected calibration
error (15 equal-count bins)119,120 and Brier score121, and outcomes also by AUROC77, AUPRC122, scaled
Brier score123 and calibration slope and intercept124. For the recalibrated forecasts, each outcome’s predicted
risks were shifted by one constant on the log-odds scale so that their mean equals the outcome’s incidence, with
the shift estimated on one half of the patients and applied to the other (two folds by patient); the calibration
error of predicted risks uses 10 equal-count bins. Coverage at precision p is the largest share of judgements,
taken in order of confidence, whose accuracy is at least p125,126, with confidence the top option’s renormalized
probability and tied confidences accepted or rejected together. Intervals are 95% document- or patient-clustered
bootstrap114,127 intervals (1,000 resamples); for balanced accuracy on the MIMIC-IV timeline, human-keyed,
ReXVQA and pathology sets, a Bayesian bootstrap128 (Dirichlet document weights) keeps every answer class
in every resample. Per-concept means use a Bayesian bootstrap over concepts (small-sample t correction) and
prognosis means a patient-level Bayesian bootstrap. Paired comparisons use the paired bootstrap, and every
system is scored on the same items.

The gated delta rule runs over the document once from a zero state, and its final recurrent state SD is kept.
Every segment then runs from a copy of SD (variable-length kernel with initial states), never from another
segment’s state. The causal convolution (four-token window) at the first three tokens of each segment is
recomputed with the document’s last tokens as left context, so no segment reads another. When several
documents share a training sequence, each starts from a zero state and its segments start from its own final
state.
